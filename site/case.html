The case for finishing the North Country Trail

Trail-wide, North Dakota to Vermont. Built from the same public record as the map: the NCTA centerline, NCTA annual reports and Form 990s, NPS publications, and the build-rate research of November 2026. No Minnesota figures appear on this page — the Minnesota case is a separate packet, and its numbers do not travel.

The short version: at the recent pace the trail finishes in roughly 40 to 80 years — the mid-2060s to about 2100 — not 5 years and not 200. Twenty years is achievable only if the recent pace roughly doubles and stays doubled. Five years would need about 300 miles of road gap closed a year, roughly six times the recent build rate.

1. The gap

1,536.15 miles are not yet built tread on the November 2026 centerline: 1,411.47 miles of road walk plus 124.68 miles of urban sidewalk. Road and urban sidewalk are not lumped — a sidewalk in town is walkable but it is not trail — but neither counts as built, so the gap is the sum.

The NCTA 2025 Annual Report gives a slightly different vintage of the same picture: 1,495 miles on-road of 4,860 total (3,365 off-road), end of 2025. The two vintages differ — the centerline measured here totals 4,871.24 miles, 11.2 miles over the report, and the off/on split differs by about 8 miles in Minnesota — and they are not reconciled by adjusting one to match the other. NCTA’s February 2026 state fact sheets give a third, lower figure: 1,418 “miles left to build.” All three are reported here rather than picked between.

By state, the road-miles burden is: Ohio 513.73, Michigan 255.05, New York 218.17, North Dakota 169.63, Minnesota 128.34, Wisconsin 64.51, Pennsylvania 43.66, Vermont 18.37.

2. The build rate — and why “built” is not “closed”

Three different numbers are routinely conflated. They are not the same:

  • Miles built — tread constructed in a year. Mean ~52 miles/year over the ten years with data (2015–2016, 2018–2025; 2017 not found), range 23–87. Recent six-year mean (2020–2025) ~59/year. This includes reroutes and reconstruction of existing off-road trail.
  • Miles certified — segments NPS has certified as meeting National Scenic Trail standards. The latest published NPS figure is from 2018 (“over 1,775 miles”); no current certified total is published. Certification lags construction and is a separate, slower count.
  • Miles of road gap closed — the only figure that moves the finish date. Gross: ~39 miles/year of roadwalk reported eliminated, 2020–2025 (234.13 miles over six years — only ~66% of the 352.75 miles built in the same period closed road gap). Net (GIS-measured): ~18.5 miles/year — on-road miles fell from 1,606.1 (end 2019) to 1,495 (end 2025), 111 miles in six years. The gap between gross and net is route-length growth and GIS re-measurement adding road miles even as old ones were closed.

Year by year, miles built: 2015: 23; 2016: 23; 2017: not found; 2018: 51; 2019: 71.4; 2020: 51; 2021: 44.75; 2022: 87; 2023: 59; 2024: 64; 2025: 47. Sources: NCTA annual and trail-progress reports, the North Star magazine, and IRS Form 990s — see data sources.

The conflation of “miles built” with “road miles closed” is the central error in this debate, and it roughly doubles the apparent speed. Any finish-date calculation must use a net rate, not the build rate.

Verdict: closer to 20 years than to 200 only under a doubling; at the recent pace, expect completion in the second half of the 21st century, and later on the net measure.

4. What limits the rate

Land and easements — the binding constraint

The NPS has never had eminent-domain authority for this trail. The only acquisition authority, granted by Congress in 2009 (National Trails System Act §5(a)(8), as amended by P.L. 111-11), permits acquiring land or interests only “with the consent of the owner” — willing seller — capped at an average of ¼ mile either side of the trail. Before 2009, §10(c) barred spending federal funds to buy land or easements for the trail at all (except one interpretive site). Fewer than 50 miles are NPS-owned or leased, all inside Pictured Rocks National Lakeshore or the St. Croix National Scenic Riverway. NPS’s first-ever land purchase for the trail — an 80-acre parcel in Michigan (Augusta Prairie, 2017) — was described by the superintendent as “monumental.” NCTA made its first-ever direct land purchase only in 2025 (8 acres on the Beaver River, PA, protecting 4 miles of existing trail).

Funding

NPS operations funding for the trail (ONPS) is about $1.5 million/year — NCTA’s FY2027 request asks only that it “stay steady for Fiscal Year 2026 and 2027 at $1,500,000,” while noting NPS “needs significantly more resources.” NCTA’s own revenue was $2.01M (2023), $2.15M (2024), $1.81M (2025). The FY2026 President’s budget proposed a ~$900M NPS cut, a 60% cut to USFS trail-maintenance funding, and deep Land and Water Conservation Fund reductions.

Volunteer capacity

The build rate is capped by roughly 1,000–1,300 volunteers a year contributing 65,000–91,000 hours, and the maintenance burden grows with every mile built (2,886–3,356 miles maintained annually). NCTA has only 15–17 paid staff. Training is a bottleneck (chainsaw certification, crew-leader training). Paid crews (~22–30 youth programs/corps partners a year) supplement volunteers, but no mileage split by builder type is published.

Permitting and land-manager approvals

Adirondack Park: NY DEC imposed a three-year moratorium on footpath construction, lifted only in 2022; the Jones Hill segment took 8 years (legal and pandemic setbacks); the Pixley Falls/Clark Hill reroute took 5 years of planning and authorization-seeking plus 3 seasons of construction to replace 8 miles of highway roadwalk. On national forests, reroutes, bridges and shelters require NEPA review. In the Boundary Waters Canoe Area Wilderness, the original authorized route crossed 70+ miles of black spruce/tamarack swamp (~40 miles of wetlands) and was never built; the reroute requires NPS NEPA review. In Ohio, the 60-mile roadwalk east of Lisbon — the longest on the trail — is still in planning.

5. Land needed

Per the 2025 Annual Report, nearly 1,500 miles are “not in a guaranteed position.” In 2025 NCTA inventoried 369 gaps and identified 75 requiring “significant, collaborative planning.” Many segments still rest on handshake agreements being converted to easements (2025: 17 use agreements/licenses, 4 easements, 1 acquisition; 2023: 12 easements; 2019: 14 easements/agreements).

6. Phasing — a prioritisation frame, not a funding ask

By state gap size, the work orders itself: Ohio (513.73 road miles) first, then Michigan (255.05), New York (218.17), North Dakota (169.63), then Minnesota (128.34), Wisconsin (64.51), Pennsylvania (43.66), Vermont (18.37). This is a frame for deciding where effort goes first. It carries no dollar figure and no ask: no trail-wide cost to build exists in the sources, and none is invented here.

7. How the trail got here — the epoch history

Public snapshots can measure line change between dated pictures of the route, not when individual miles were built. The defensible numbers are the net changes:

  • 1982 → 2005: net +1,896 miles of route line (gross “new” 2,436 mi is an upper bound that mixes genuine construction with re-routes, re-digitization, and dataset coverage change — it is not miles built). The 1982 baseline is thin (999.7 mi of “existing” trail in a plan-route digitization), so the net is a lower bound on line growth in that period.
  • 2005 → 2026: net +1,907 miles (gross “new” 2,694 mi, again an upper bound). The headline trap is Minnesota: 781 mi “new” there, but the 2005 dataset holds only 158.0 mi in Minnesota — the Superior Hiking Trail (300 mi) is absent from the 2005 file. Most of the Minnesota “new” is coverage change, not miles built. The clean additions are Vermont (70.6 mi, the 2019 extension, zero “gone”) and North Dakota (274.9 mi new).

The independent build-rate record (~52 mi/yr built 2015–2025) implies roughly 1,100 miles of genuine construction over 2005–2026, about 40% of the gross “new” figure; the rest is the line moving or being drawn differently.

The 2016 gap. There is no dated snapshot between May 2005 and November 2026: the 2016 NY/PA centerline (NPS IRMA Ref 2229138) carries only a metadata PDF, no retrievable geometry. The 2005→2026 epoch cannot be split at 2016 except via OpenStreetMap history, and only for Michigan (net +263 mi mapped route, 2020→today — mapping dates, not build dates).

8. What this case does not claim

  • No trail-wide dollar cost figure. None exists in the sources checked. This page does not invent one.
  • No completion date is promised. The scenarios are arithmetic on stated rates, not a plan. NCTA’s 2025–2030 Strategic Plan sets no miles-per-year goal and no completion date; its breakthrough goal is trail protection and land stewardship (“create and deliver the forever route”). The 2020–2023 plan aimed to complete 150 miles in priority areas over three years (50/year); the October 2022 executive-director statement of “1,700 more miles over the next two decades” (~85/year built) is an aspiration, not a formal target.
  • Minnesota numbers are not in this case. The Minnesota economics and phasing packet is a separate, draft document; its figures (including any dollar ask) are Minnesota-only and are not carried here.
